How machines learn · a hands-on guide

Fit.Transform.Predict.

Here's the secret nobody tells you at the AI hype party. Most of the AI you hear about today, from your spam filter to ChatGPT, is built on three steps. It learns patterns from examples, it turns messy input into tidy numbers, and it takes a guess. In scikit-learn, a popular machine learning toolkit, they're literally called fit, transform and predict. You're about to do all three yourself.

No maths degree required. Pinky promise. Code nerds can hit Show code up top.

Chapter 1fit()

Learning is just finding the pattern

Meet a tiny ice-cream shop. Every day it writes down two things: how hot it was and how many ice creams it sold. Each dot is one day.

"Fitting" means finding the rule that matches the dots best. Here the rule is a straight line. The computer tries a line, measures how badly it misses each dot (those red dashes are its regrets), and nudges the line to regret a little less. Repeat until it can't do better.

Full honesty: for a straight line there's a shortcut formula that jumps straight to the best answer, and that's what scikit-learn uses. The guess-and-nudge method (called gradient descent) is what big models use, so that's what "Watch it learn" shows.

Play with it: click empty space to add a day. Drag any dot around and watch the line chase it. Make a scorching day with zero sales and see one weird example drag the whole thing.

Then try "Your turn". Drag the yellow handles and try to beat the machine. Spoiler: it's annoyingly good at this.

In generative AIChatGPT-style models get fit the same basic way: guess, measure the miss, nudge, repeat. The big difference is scale. Our shop tunes 2 numbers from 10 days. Those models tune billions of numbers (the biggest go past a trillion) from trillions of words' worth of text. That happens ahead of time, at the AI company, and can cost many millions of dollars.

Our model has two numbers. It ain't much, but it's honest work.

Show me the code 
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

X = [[13], [16], [19], ...]   # temperature each day
y = [52, 88, 110, ...]         # ice creams sold

model = LinearRegression()
model.fit(X, y)                     # learn the line
print(model.coef_, model.intercept_) # the two numbers it learned
Chapter 2transform()

Real data is messy, so we tidy up first

Real notebooks have gaps. Someone forgot to write the temperature. Someone wrote hot!! instead of a number, which is relatable but not helpful. Most classic models only speak numbers, so they take one look and say: computer says no. (A few, like some tree-based models, can cope with blanks on their own. Most can't.)

So you pick a cleaning strategy. Each one costs you something:

Drop the messy rows. Everything left is true, but you threw away data.
Fill with the average or middle value. You keep every row, but you just made numbers up.
Let a model guess the missing temperature from that day's sales. Often the smartest move, because it uses the other clues in the row.

Keep an eye on Friday. 300 ice creams sold, so it was clearly a scorcher. Fill it with the average and it suddenly claims 25.6 °C. Friday deserved better.

In generative AIThat last option, predict the missing piece from its surroundings, isn't just a cleaning trick. It's the core idea behind how generative AI gets trained. Hold that thought until chapter 7.

Chapter 3predict()

Predicting is using the pattern on something new

Line learned. Now the shop can ask the useful question: "Tomorrow's forecast says 30 °C. How many ice creams do we stock?" The model just reads the answer off its line.

This uses the line you shaped in chapter 1. Go back, add some chaos, come back here. The guesses change.

Now break it. Drag the temperature down to 2 °C or up to 48 °C. The model has never seen days like that, yet it answers with full confidence. Go cold enough and it predicts a negative number of ice creams. The shop would have to buy ice creams back from people. The model sees no problem with this.

In generative AIThis is a close cousin of "hallucination". Ask a chatbot about something it barely saw in training and it can still produce a smooth, confident answer. Modern chatbots are trained to say "I'm not sure" more often, but underneath, predict always produces something.

Chapter 4Pipeline

Chain it all together. Learn once, reuse forever.

Real systems glue the steps into a pipeline. Plot twist: the cleaning steps learn too. "Fill with average" has to learn what the average is. "Rescale" learns what counts as hot or cold for this particular shop. So fit runs through the whole chain once. After that, each new day just flows through and every step reuses what it remembered. Work smarter, not harder.

from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

pipe = make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(), StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())
pipe.fit(X_notebook, y_sales)   # every step learns, once
pipe.predict([[np.nan]])        # a blank day: every step reuses what it learned
In generative AIA chatbot is a pipeline too: your text → chopped into tokens → tokens turned into numbers → the model → pick a word → text back. Every part was fit ahead of time, even the chopping part. While you chat, you're only triggering transform and predict.
Chapter 5transform() for words

Language models can't actually read. Words become numbers first.

Models only do arithmetic, so text has to become numbers. Step one: chop the text into tokens, which are common words or word chunks. Each token gets an ID number.

The list of chunks was itself learned from a mountain of text. Chunks that show up a lot get their own token. So the tokenizer is a transformer that was fit, same energy as the "fill with average" step. (The tokenizer on this page is a simplified toy with made-up ID numbers. Real ones split words a bit differently, but the idea is the same.)

Type a typo. "love" is one familiar token. "lovee" gets chopped into love + e. No crash, unlike classic ML choking on hot!!. It just gets slightly odd pieces. Invent a word and watch it shatter into crumbs.

Step two: each token becomes a long list of numbers called an embedding. Think of it as GPS coordinates for meaning. Words used in similar ways end up as neighbours. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of numbers. The map here is hand-drawn to show the idea in two dimensions, not taken from a real model.

And you can do maths with meaning. Take "hot", subtract "summer", add "winter". Where do you land? Big brain time, try the calculator under the map. On our hand-drawn map it works neatly. In real models it works some of the time: the famous example is king minus man plus woman landing near queen, though it's messier than the headlines suggest.

Chapter 6predict(), on repeat

Generative AI is predict, over and over and over

This is the big one. A language model only ever predicts the next token, which is roughly the next word or chunk of a word. That's it. To write a whole answer, it predicts one word, glues it on, and predicts again. That loop is the "generative" part.

Two details make it work:

1. It predicts chances, not one answer. Every possible next token gets a probability. scikit-learn calls this kind of output predict_proba.

2. Then it rolls weighted dice. "Temperature" controls the dice. Turn it down and it nearly always takes the top word: safe, a bit boring. Crank it up and long shots get a real chance: creative, occasionally unhinged. That's a big reason the same question can get you a different answer each time.

Our toy was fit on the ten sentences below and only looks at the last word. You asked for ChatGPT? We have ChatGPT at home. Real models read thousands of words at once (some far more), which is why they stay on topic and ours wanders off to talk about cats.

Chapter 7filling in the blanks

Filling in missing values was the whole trick all along

Remember "let a model guess the missing temperature" from chapter 2? Generative AI is built on exactly that. To train one, take real text, hide a word, make the model guess it, check, nudge, repeat billions of times. Nobody has to label anything. The text is its own answer key. That's the first and biggest stage of training. Chatbots then get extra rounds of training with human feedback so they follow instructions and behave.

GPT-style models always hide the next word. Others, like BERT, hide words in the middle. Many image generators (called diffusion models) do it with pictures: they bury an image in noise and learn to dig it back out.

Typos: language models have no typo-cleaning step. They cope because their training text was absolutely full of typos. The model has seen things. So it learned that "lovee" next to "pizza" means love. The robustness was learned, not programmed in.

Hallucination: filling a blank is always a guess. Ask about something that doesn't exist and it fills the blank anyway. Trust me bro energy.

Chapter 8the big picture

Same blueprint, wildly different scale

Where the blueprint bends

You never call fit

With a chatbot, the learning already happened. "Fine-tuning" is a smaller second round of fitting on specialised examples, like teaching our model about one particular beach kiosk.

Your prompt is a temporary fit

Show a chatbot three examples in your message and it follows the pattern. Nothing from training changes; it adapts only for this chat. That's called in-context learning, and classic models can't do it.

Predict became a loop

Classic predict hands back one answer. Generative AI calls predict once per token, often hundreds or thousands of times, feeding each output back in. That loop plus the dice roll is what makes it "generative".

Cleaning became learned

In classic ML a person writes the cleaning rules. In generative AI, coping with typos, slang and gaps comes from examples. More flexible, less predictable.

Wait. So it's all just fit, transform, predict?
Always has been.

Okay, the fine print. That's a meme, not a textbook definition. Fit, transform, predict is the blueprint for machine learning, which powers most of the AI you hear about today. It isn't the whole story of AI. For decades, AI mostly meant hand-written rules and search (think classic chess engines), with nothing learned from data. Some methods, like reinforcement learning (how AlphaGo learned to play Go), mix learning and acting in one ongoing loop. And today's AI products wrap extra machinery around the model, such as web search, memory and tools.

This page simplifies on purpose. Every model on it is a tiny toy that runs in your browser, and illustrative numbers are labelled as such.
